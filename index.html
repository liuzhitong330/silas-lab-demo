Exploratory Analyses Inspired by Silas Lab Publications

Cathy Liu · June 2026

Motivation

The Silas Lab studies the phage-host immune arms race through the lens of accessory genes (AGs) — small, fast-evolving, non-essential phage genes that the lab treats as a systematic readout of which host defenses matter most in real, wild bacterial strains rather than lab-adapted ones. What drew me in is the lab's 2026 Nature paper showing that an anti-CRISPR protein doesn't just block Cas12 protein activity — it triggers translation-dependent degradation of cas12 mRNA itself. Most anti-CRISPRs studied to date work at the protein level (steric blocking, allosteric inhibition); finding one that reaches back to disable the transcript is a different class of mechanism, and it immediately raises the question of how common transcript-level anti-defense is across the AG repertoire.

I'm also struck by the lab's "lose-lose bind" hypothesis: that programmed cell death (PCD) / abortive infection systems may have evolved specifically to sense the AGs that suppress host immunity, so that a phage either loses a useful immune-suppressive function or risks triggering host suicide. That's a sharp, falsifiable idea, and the lab's transposon-suppressor "layer-peeling" approach for wild strains seems like exactly the right tool to test it. Below, I used LLM-assisted literature synthesis to organize candidate hypotheses across the lab's recent papers, then manually refined them into the specific, testable questions shown here, plus a small mock screen analysis in the lab's own idiom (pooled AG screens, not the RNA-seq workflows I usually default to).

Experiment 1: Research Gap Identification

I used an LLM (claude-opus-4-8) to synthesize five recent Silas Lab papers plus the lab's research statement, then reviewed and trimmed its output down to the two gaps below that I found most specific and testable with tools the lab already has.

1. How does the PCD "guard" actually sense anti-defense AGs?

The lab's Mol Cell 2025 paper shows that PCD/abortive-infection systems sense phage immune inhibitors (anti-CRISPRs, anti-restriction proteins) and trigger host suicide — but not how. Does the guard detect the inhibitor protein directly, or the downstream consequence of inhibition (e.g., a freed defense complex)? Test: pair the AG-expression PCD screen with AP-MS to ask whether the PCD sensor co-purifies with the AG itself or with its host target, then use separation-of- function AG mutants (binds target but doesn't inhibit it) to see if PCD still fires.

2. What do broad-spectrum anti-defense AGs actually target?

The lab's Nat Commun 2025 paper on anti-restriction AGs hints that some AGs counteract multiple, structurally unrelated defenses at once — suggesting they hit a shared host node rather than each defense protein individually. Test: screen a panel of strains each carrying a single, defined defense system (CRISPR, R-M, CBASS, Thoeris), score each AG's anti-defense spectrum, then use AP-MS on the broad-spectrum hits to find the shared target.

Experiment 2: Iterative Hypothesis Refinement

I picked the single most surprising finding in the lab's recent work — the translation-dependent cas12 mRNA decay mechanism — and used the LLM to draft candidate mechanisms, then pushed back with a confound I identified myself (is this just nonspecific overexpression stress?) before asking for one falsifiable prediction that could distinguish the two models using tools already in the lab's screening framework. The exchange below shows that refinement process.

Turn 1 · Prompt

The Silas Lab (Marino et al., Nature 2026) found that a phage anti-CRISPR triggers translation-dependent degradation of cas12 mRNA, not just protein blocking. Propose 2–3 concrete mechanisms for how that could work.

Claude

Three candidate mechanisms, all requiring active translation: (1) the Acr binds the nascent Cas12 polypeptide and stalls the ribosome, exposing the mRNA to host surveillance nucleases; (2) the Acr itself docks near the ribosome and cleaves cas12 mRNA directly, toxin–antitoxin style; (3) the Acr recognizes a co-translational epitope on nascent Cas12 and recruits a host degradation complex (e.g. RNase E) to the polysome. Shared prediction: blocking translation should uncouple Acr expression from decay.

Turn 2 · Pushback

Confound: this could just be generic ribosome-stalling stress from overexpressing any phage protein, not a mechanism specifically evolved to target cas12. How would you rule that out?

Claude

Three controls: (1) transcriptome-wide half-life profiling — the specific model predicts cas12 is a lone outlier, the stress model predicts a broad cohort of destabilized transcripts; (2) a catalytically-dead Acr mutant at matched expression — if decay disappears, the effect requires Acr function, not just protein burden; (3) a reporter swap — fuse the cas12 sequence to GFP and an unrelated gene to the cas12 promoter, to test whether specificity tracks the transcript itself or just the locus.

Turn 3 · Mechanistic prediction

Give one falsifiable prediction that follows from the specific model but not the stress model, testable with ribosome profiling in the lab's existing wild-strain panel.

Claude

Prediction: ribosome profiling will show a sharp, reproducible stall peak at one fixed codon within cas12, coincident with a discrete cleavage site — not the diffuse, expression-scaled stalling the stress model predicts. Across Acr⁺ wild strains, that stall coordinate should map to the same conserved cas12 sequence element; if instead it varies by strain or tracks expression level, the stress model wins.

Mini Analysis: Pooled AG Screen Analysis (Tn-seq / count-based fitness)

This is a synthetic toy dataset, not Silas Lab data; the goal is to show how I think about pooled-screen structure, QC, and the statistics that connect a wet-lab screen to an interpretable result — not to claim a real finding.

To mirror the lab's actual experimental idiom — pooled screens across many wild bacterial strains, not single-strain RNA-seq — I mocked up a count-based AG fitness screen: each AG in a pooled library is scored by its log2 fold-change in phage-infectivity rescue (resistant strain becomes susceptible when the AG is expressed) versus its enrichment as a PCD trigger (susceptible strain dies when the AG is expressed, phage-independent). Synthetic counts, seeded for reproducibility.

R: count-based fitness scoring (edgeR, same framework used for Tn-seq/CRISPR screens)

library(edgeR)
library(tidyverse)

# counts: AGs x {screen replicates x condition}, e.g. AG library in
# resistant strain +/- phage, and PCD screen in susceptible strain alone
counts <- read.delim("ag_screen_counts.tsv", row.names = 1)
design <- model.matrix(~condition, data = sample_info)

dge <- DGEList(counts = counts, group = sample_info$condition)
dge <- calcNormFactors(dge)
dge <- estimateDisp(dge, design)

fit <- glmQLFit(dge, design)
qlf <- glmQLFTest(fit, coef = "conditionphage_selected")
res <- topTags(qlf, n = Inf)$table

# anti-defense AGs: positive logFC (rescue infectivity under phage selection)
# PCD-trigger AGs: negative logFC (depleted/lethal without phage, in PCD screen)
anti_defense <- res %>% filter(logFC > 1, FDR < 0.05)
pcd_triggers <- res %>% filter(logFC < -1, FDR < 0.05)

Python: parallel screen ingestion across wild strain panel

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# one count table per wild strain; AGs as rows, replicate timepoints as columns
strains = ["strain_01", "strain_02", "strain_03"]  # ... up to n=125 wild isolates

def fitness_score(counts_t0, counts_tf):
    # log2 enrichment of each AG relative to library-wide median
    lfc = np.log2((counts_tf + 1) / (counts_t0 + 1))
    return lfc - np.median(lfc)

screen_results = {}
for strain in strains:
    t0 = pd.read_csv(f"{strain}_t0_counts.csv", index_col=0)
    tf = pd.read_csv(f"{strain}_tf_counts.csv", index_col=0)
    screen_results[strain] = fitness_score(t0["count"], tf["count"])

# strains cluster by defense repertoire richness (PCA below) before AG analysis
fitness_df = pd.DataFrame(screen_results)

Why This Matters

  • Wild-strain context is the whole point. The defenses the lab cares about are often masked by redundancy in lab strains; their layer-peeling transposon screens and parallel wild-strain panels are the right setting for an RA who can combine careful wet-lab execution with enough computational literacy to understand pooled-screen outputs and flag what's worth following up.
  • The lose-lose bind hypothesis is testable computationally before it's tested experimentally. Overlaying anti-defense fitness landscapes against PCD-trigger fitness landscapes is fundamentally a data-integration problem, but it only matters if the underlying pooled screens were built and tracked carefully in the first place.
  • Transcript-level anti-defense (the cas12 mRNA decay mechanism) opens a new data modality. If this generalizes, the lab will need to pair ribosome profiling and RNA-seq sample prep with the existing screen infrastructure — bench work and downstream interpretation have to move together.

How I Would Contribute as a Wet-Lab RA

  • Molecular cloning and library construction: learn and execute the lab's AG cloning workflows, from individual constructs to pooled library generation, with careful attention to representation and complexity.
  • Bacterial strain work and sample tracking: maintain precise sample tracking across parallel pooled screens run in dozens to hundreds of wild bacterial strains — transformations, phage challenges, and timepoint collections where mislabeling even one sample breaks the whole comparison.
  • NGS sample prep and nucleic acid handling: support DNA/RNA purification and library prep for the pooled screen sequencing and any follow-up RNA-seq/Ribo-seq work, with the QC habits to catch degraded or low-complexity samples before they reach sequencing.
  • Documentation and troubleshooting: keep Benchling-style protocol and troubleshooting records so screen conditions, failed attempts, and strain-specific quirks are reproducible and shareable across the lab.

Concrete Computational Contributions

  • Build and maintain AG screen analysis pipelines (edgeR/MAGeCK-style fitness scoring) that scale across the lab's growing wild-strain panel, with automated QC for library representation and replicate correlation.
  • Develop cross-screen integration tools to overlay phage-dependent anti-defense and phage-independent PCD fitness landscapes per AG — directly operationalizing the lose-lose bind hypothesis as a testable statistical model.
  • Support ribosome profiling / 5′P-end RNA-seq analysis (Ribo-seq QC, stall-site calling, decay intermediate mapping) to follow up on the cas12 mRNA decay mechanism and test whether it generalizes across the Acr-encoding wild-strain panel.
  • Support AP-MS data analysis for identifying host targets of broad-spectrum anti-defense AGs, including interactome comparison across strains to distinguish shared-hub vs. modular mechanisms.
